# COLD CALL — PLAYBOOK B — THE TRIPLE BYPASS

**THE STRATEGY IN ONE LINE:** say the least that will get you transferred, and only give
more when they force it out of you.

Every other gatekeeper approach front-loads information — name, company, reason — and each
piece is another thing to screen on. This one starts at the minimum and escalates ONLY under
pressure. You never volunteer a step. Most calls end at step one.

**Why it out-bypasses the alternative:** the gatekeeper's screen is triggered by CATEGORY
("who are you and what do you sell"), not by identity. Giving a first name and an instruction
gives them nothing to screen. Nothing false is ever said — you simply don't answer questions
nobody asked.

**The cost, stated honestly:** it works by sounding like someone who belongs. If they ask
directly whether you've spoken before, you answer honestly and lose the frame. Playbook A
(the Hold Play) trades bypass rate for durability. This one trades durability for bypass rate.
On a first-ever dial into a business, B wins.

---

## THE THREE STEPS — never skip ahead, never volunteer the next one

### STEP 1 — THE SLIDE-BY (start here, every time)
First name only. No company. No title. No reason.

> "Hey, could you get me over to [OWNER FIRST NAME]? It's [YOUR FIRST NAME]."

**Delivery is the whole thing.** It must be one continuous instruction with no pause at the
end. The classic failure is giving a first name and then STOPPING — the pause is the tell
every gatekeeper is trained on. Do not trail off. Do not go up at the end like a question.

Most calls end here. If they transfer, say nothing else.

### STEP 2 — CONTEXT (only if challenged)
They ask what it's about, or who you're with. Give a reason WITHOUT a pitch, then re-issue
the instruction.

> "I work with a few other [owners / shops / contractors] in the [area]. Would you let him
> know it's [FIRST NAME]?"

Still no product. Still no category. The re-ask at the end is what keeps it moving.

### STEP 3 — SOCIAL PROOF (only if pushed a second time)
Now, and only now, the company name — carried on peer proof so it lands as a known quantity
rather than a cold vendor.

> "We help a couple of other [junk removal outfits / roofers] in [area] with their
> after-hours calls. It's [YOUR NAME] with [COMPANY]."

**NEVER name a client you have not actually been given.** If there are none, use the shape
without the name: "a couple of other outfits in the area." The shape is what does the work.

---

## THE FOUR RULES UNDER ALL THREE STEPS

1. **INSTRUCT, DON'T ASK.** "Could you get me over to…" / "Would you let him know…" You are
   directing traffic, not requesting permission.
2. **NEVER PITCH THE GATEKEEPER.** The pitch IS the screen trigger. Nothing you say to them
   can help you; it can only get you blocked.
3. **DON'T GO DARK ON THE COMPANY WHEN ASKED DIRECTLY.** Obscurity is itself a tell — reps
   who dodge the company name get screened at a HIGHER rate. Step 3 exists for this. Give it,
   wrapped in peer proof.
4. **NEVER SOUND APOLOGETIC.** "I was hoping to speak with…" / "I'm not sure if this is the
   right person…" broadcast a low-value call before the sentence finishes. Uncertainty is the
   single loudest screening signal there is.

---

## WHEN THEY DON'T TRANSFER — give them a decision they can actually make

A yes/no ("is he in?") defaults to no. A ROUTING question is one they're qualified to answer
and want to answer:

> "Is that something [OWNER] handles directly, or does that sit with someone else?"

Other openings that recruit instead of fight:
> "Maybe you could help me out for a second?"
> "Is there a time of day he's usually easiest to catch?"

**Every tactic must pass all three:** it is TRUE (you could repeat it word for word when the
owner picks up), it gives them a DECISION, and it REDUCES their work. Anything failing one of
those is what gets you screened.

### "He's not available"
> "That makes sense. Is there a time of day he's usually easiest to catch?"

And the one nobody expects, which turns the blocker into the meeting:
> "Can you and I meet?"

### "Send an email and I'll pass it on"
Never trade. Comply AND extract:
> "Happy to. Could I get his direct address? And if I don't hear back in a few days, would it
> be okay if I tried again?"

**Always leave with something: an email, a name, or a best time to call back.**

---

## BURNED TACTICS — do not use, they cost more than they buy
- Claiming you were "asked to follow up" when you weren't
- Name-dropping a colleague you never spoke to
- Any invented prior meeting, prior call, or referral
- Hiding your company when asked straight out

These buy thirty seconds of access at the price of the whole call — and the owner is the one
who finds out. The Slide-By works WITHOUT any of them, which is the entire point.

---

## SKIP THE GATE ENTIRELY WHERE YOU CAN
- **Ask for a different department.** "Could you connect me with dispatch, please?" Internal
  transfers aren't screened.
- **Call when the gate isn't there.** Before 8am, after 5pm, 12–1. For trades, midday is the
  WORST window — the owner is on a job.
- **Dial the mobile.** Owner-operators publish cells on Google Business, trucks, yard signs.
  There is no gatekeeper on a cell.

---

## ONCE THE OWNER PICKS UP — you have 7 seconds

**NEVER "did I catch you at a bad time"** — lowest-converting opener measured, ~0.9%.

Open non-invasively. Get him describing his own situation before he classifies you as a sales
call. Do not introduce the product yet.

The softener is load-bearing — it removes the freedom-threat that triggers resistance:
> "I'm not sure this would even make sense for you, but…"

Then the reason, plainly, and a **"because" about HIS problem, not your product.** He gives up
his time for his reasons, never yours.

**Talk about 55% of the time.** A cold call is not a discovery call. Note his pain and do NOT
solve it here — unresolved pain is what buys the appointment.

---

## THE APPOINTMENT IS THE ONLY WIN

1. **Name** — "Hi [name]."
2. **Identify** — name and company.
3. **Reason** — "The reason I'm calling is to set up a time."
4. **Bridge** — the "because," in his world.
5. **ASK A SPECIFIC TIME — THEN SHUT UP.**

**No pause before the ask; total silence after it.** Every word after the ask reopens the
decision.

- **TRY FOR RIGHT NOW FIRST.** "Would you be open if I called you back in twenty minutes?"
  Beats a calendar slot and almost nobody asks for it.
- **Two options, never open-ended.** "Tuesday at 10 or Thursday at 2?"
- **On the first refusal, shrink it — don't re-pitch.** "Fair enough. Two minutes, right now?"
- **Commit small, then reveal.** Yes to "two minutes," then it's twenty.
- **Give them the out, at the ask only.** "…and if it's not for you, just say so, no problem."
- **Lock it:** specific day AND time · timezone out loud · callback number · what number shows
  on their phone · email for the invite · their name.

**"Send me some info" is a brush-off, not an objection.** A scripted exit. Don't trade the
appointment for it.

---

## VOICE
- **Absolute certainty** — the Slide-By only works in this tone. It carries the whole step.
- **Implied obviousness** — the transfer is self-evident, already agreed. Tone, never a claim.
- **The reasonable man** — "sound fair enough?" on the ask.
- **Utter sincerity** — the honest negative ("I don't have an appointment").
- **Mystery and intrigue** — step 2's context line. Drop the voice slightly.
- **I care** — the owner's pain only. Never at the gate.

## NEVER SAY
- "Did I catch you at a bad time" · "Is now a bad time"
- "I was hoping to…" · "I'm not sure if you're the right person…"
- Any claim of prior contact that did not happen
- A pitch to anyone who is not the decision maker
- A price before the appointment is booked
